# Business Administration Basics

Reference knowledge for the business side of running a network and a company. General
principles, not legal or financial advice; consult a professional for decisions.

## The three core financial statements

The income statement shows revenue minus expenses over a period, ending in net income.
The balance sheet is a snapshot: assets equal liabilities plus equity. The cash flow
statement reconciles net income to actual cash moved, split into operating, investing,
and financing activities. Profit is an accrual concept; cash is what keeps the lights
on. A company can be profitable on paper and still run out of cash.

## Runway and burn

Burn is net cash spent per month; runway is cash on hand divided by burn — how many
months remain before you must raise or reach breakeven. Track it monthly. A raise takes
time, so start well before runway runs short. Unit economics — the contribution margin
of one customer or one node — decide whether growth helps or hurts.

## Fundraising instruments

Early-stage companies often raise on a SAFE or a convertible note: money now that
converts to equity at a later priced round, often with a valuation cap or discount. A
priced round sells shares at a set valuation. Non-cash consideration (contributing
compute, IP, or services for equity) is still consideration and must be valued and
documented. Dilution is the reduction in ownership percentage as new shares are issued.

## Pricing and tiers

A common structure is a free tier to drive adoption, a paid individual tier, and an
enterprise tier with a contract and volume terms. Price to the value delivered, not the
cost incurred. Be precise and never overstate traction: state real numbers, and let the
figures carry the case rather than adjectives.

## Cap table and equity

The capitalization table records who owns what: founders, employees (usually via an
option pool that vests over time, often four years with a one-year cliff), and
investors. Keep it clean and current; a messy cap table complicates every future round.

## Operating cadence

Set a small number of measurable goals per period and review them on a regular cadence.
Write decisions down so they survive turnover. Separate the metric that matters (the one
that reflects real value) from vanity metrics that only look good. Keep a paper trail:
what was decided, why, and what evidence supported it.
